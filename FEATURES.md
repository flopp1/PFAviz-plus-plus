# PFAviz++ in detail

What each part of PFAviz++ does, with the measurements behind it. For a short overview, see the [README](README.md).
**Loading**
- Files load on a background thread with a progress and statistics popup: per-stage times, throughput, memory in use and its peak.
- The loader is memory-mapped and multi-threaded. A 100 M-note file loads in about 1.9 s on a 6-core CPU; files that do not fit in memory are read once, at the disk's sequential speed.
- Events are stored LZ4-compressed (liblz4's HC: 0.2–0.4 bytes per event on black MIDIs), and notes that can never show are dropped: a 1.1 billion-note, 6.3 GB file takes about 270 MB once loaded.
- Peak memory while loading stays close to what the song takes once loaded. The event stream is built a window at a time, and the count pass works out where every note ends, so pairing finishes each note at once instead of holding back every later note of its key until its note-off. *Night of Nights* (17.7 GB, 2.3 billion notes): peak 10.4 → 4.4 GB, 3.9 GB once loaded; *Destruction 321 SE*: 2.7 → 1.5 GB. The notes' ticks are kept as compact records per track and merged a window at a time, so songs with billions of distinct ticks fit too: *nut3* (1 billion ticks) peaks at 3.5 GB instead of 21.5 GB.
- **Notes are paired once, while counting** (2026-11-15). The count pass already applied the original's pairing rules track by track; now every note's end comes from there in a compact channel (0.01–0.4 bytes a note), and the pairing stage only sorts the notes into place. On 14 large black MIDIs (0.36–2.0 GB) every one loads faster (by 4–51 %) and peaks lower, most where notes are held long: *Dead Zone Logizomechanophobia Mode* (267 M notes) loads in 4.5 s instead of 9.2 s, peaking at 0.4 GB instead of 4.3 GB; Paprika's *Aua Ah* merge in 4.5 s instead of 6.7 s, peaking at 0.6 GB instead of 1.5 GB; a 451-track Paprika edit peaks at 0.35 GB instead of 0.84 GB. Files of many short tracks gain least: the count pass does more work.
- **A track that holds most of the file is counted on all threads**: a quick pass cuts it at event boundaries, the pieces are counted at once and joined in order. *Window* (one 1.37 GB track) counts in about 3 s instead of 5.6 s, and loads in 4.7 s instead of 7.7 s.
- **Compact visual store** (Performance tab, on by default) keeps the notes LZ4-compressed in groups of 2048 per key, decoding only the groups a frame draws: 20–36 % less memory for the notes (*Night of Nights*: 2,260 → 1,635 MB), with the same pixels. Frames read from it while scrubbing (a jump every frame) cost 0–25 % more CPU, still under about 6 ms on *Night of Nights* and *Destruction 321 SE*.
- Notes transposed past key 127 (a common black MIDI artefact) are read as stock Piano From Above reads them and dropped, instead of throwing the rest of their track out of step; velocities and controller values past 127 are clamped.
- **Weird notes end at their own note-off** (always: the option to end them at their first note-off is gone). When more than 10 notes stack up on one channel and key in a track, the original pairs the next note-off with the latest unpaired note of that key on *any* channel; a note taken from another channel's stack is then paired again by its own channel's note-off. The original draws it to that later note-off but stops drawing it (and unlights its key) at the first one, so it seems to end early, often the moment it reaches the keyboard. Here it lasts to its later note-off, which in *Night of Nights* is its own channel's for 1,146 of its 1,174 such notes (a note-off on another channel cannot end it). Songs with them also load with one pairing pass instead of two (*Night of Nights*, from an SSD: loading 76 s, pairing 53 s).
- **Exact tempo timing** (Misc tab, on by default; for songs opened next): each tick's time is the exact sum of the tempo segments before it, rounded down once. The original rounds each tempo segment down to whole microseconds on its own, so a segment shorter than a microsecond takes no time: a song counting up its tempo every 7 ticks at a division of 8 collapses into an instant once the tempo reaches 1 µs a beat (60 million BPM), and runs at half speed at 2 µs (30 million BPM). Off, times are the original's to the microsecond (it differs by under a microsecond per tempo change, which can add up on songs with many of them).
- **Layered compressed files: notes without a note-off last until the song's end** (Misc tab, on by default). Layered black MIDIs (a MIDI compressed in two or more layers, such as `.mid.xz.xz`) often leave the note-offs out to fit more notes, and expect the player to end them at the end of the file. Other songs are drawn as the original draws them, which never draws such notes.
- Archives and compressed files open the MIDI file inside; an archive holding several asks which one to open, with their sizes.
- **Deduplicate repeated tracks** (Performance tab, off by default): identical tracks are loaded once and drawn and counted as many times as they appear, in plain files and archives alike. Layered compressed files (a MIDI cut into pieces, each compressed, repeated and compressed again, several layers deep, so that a few MB expand to terabytes) are read as their distinct pieces, so files of trillions of notes load in a minute or two: *NoiseMidi* (94 trillion notes) in about 2 minutes. "Assume the rest of a layer repeats when its size fits" (on by default) skips decoding the rest of a layer once its pattern repeats; it is exact unless a different piece happens to have the same size. Duplicates are played once, which can sound quieter on a synth that stacks identical notes.
- 7z (LZMA2) and xz files compressed on several threads decompress on the loading threads (Performance tab), instead of on one.
- Tempo events of 0 (damaged files) are ignored instead of stopping time.
- A file's time division is read as SMPTE only for real frame rates (24, 25, 29.97 and 30 fps), so files with a very fine PPQN (such as 61440) are no longer squeezed to time 0.
- Closing or switching songs no longer freezes the window, however large the song: the old one is freed on a thread of its own.

**Drawing**
- Each frame finds, per key, only the notes that own a pixel row, so drawing cost depends on the window size, not the note count. There is no note limit.
- Consecutive one-colour rects of a key that meet edge to edge are drawn as one, with the same pixels. In dense passages, where every pixel row has its own note, this removes almost all of them (the end of *rftmis*: 69,400 rects per frame → 373, frame time 1.55 → 0.87 ms).
- An optional **LOD cache** (on by default) keeps, for the current window size and note speed, only the notes that can show, built in the background around the playhead. Dense songs draw several times faster with the same pixels.
- Optional **GPU instancing for notes** (Performance tab, off by default): each note goes to the GPU as one 32-byte instance instead of two quads (160 bytes), and a vertex shader builds its rects. It is not pixel-exact: over the test harness's 90,000 frames, 171 differ from the original, each by one colour level (out of 255) along one or two pixel columns of a note's gradient; note edges are never off. With a long note span it saves about a quarter of the frame time (rftmis at 3 s: 0.49 → 0.36 ms median); at short spans it is about even.
- **Hidden channels** get their own drawing data, built in the background ("Applying hidden channels..." until ready), so hiding most of a black MIDI's tracks makes it faster.
- **128 keys** by default ("Show all 88 keys" is still there).
- **Clean drawing** (Notes & keys > Note drawing, on by default): leaves out three rounding quirks of the original's note drawing. Each can be turned off on its own under Advanced: every note at least one pixel tall; short notes kept on screen from the top edge until they reach the keyboard (the original can drop such notes at either end); and note edges rounded evenly, so lower layers never show past notes above. With some of them off, the switch shows partly checked.
- **Tick-based view** (Misc tab, off by default): notes are spaced by their ticks instead of their time, a quarter note always as tall as half a second at 120 BPM, so a tempo change no longer stretches or squeezes the notes; the playhead moves through each beat at the song's own pace. Measure lines follow the same spacing, and key fades and the hit line keep real time.
- The volume, playback speed and note speed sliders are wider than the original's, and the playback and note speed sliders are each followed by a box with the exact value (always shown).
- The toolbar's sliders are wider than the original's, and the speed sliders' ranges are stretched with them, so a pixel moves each as far as before: playback speed from 5% to 269% with 100% in the middle (as the original's), its right half moving as the original did and its left half going down to 5%. The **note speed slider** is logarithmic, 0.06 (the default) in the middle, about 535 at the left and 0.00072 at the right (0.0001x to 83x in the box beside it). The boxes beside the speed sliders are wide enough for the longest value either slider gives.
- The boxes beside the playback and note speed sliders take far more than the sliders (their tips say so): playback speed from 0.001% to 100,000%, note speed from 0.00006x to 1,200x (a third of the view spanning 0.05 ms to 1000 s); the keys' speed steps reach the same limits. The sliders stay at their ends beyond their ranges. Played with rendered audio on 9KX2 at each end and both together (1000x with the whole song in view, 0.00001x with the shortest view), it kept its frame rate and nothing broke.

**Playback**
- Audio runs on its own decoder and sender threads, off a microsecond clock, independent of the frame rate. The output is chosen in Preferences > Audio, a subtab each: **KDMAPI** (OmniMIDI and other synths that provide it; WinMM's device when none is installed), **WinMM** (a MIDI output device from the list) or **Pre-Render** (below). Choosing a subtab, or a device in the list, switches to it at once, without Apply. The synth is opened once and stays open across songs, so it loads its soundfonts only at the first song (or when the output setting changes); a song that closes leaves its channels reset.
- Every event is sent by default. Optional limits (Performance > Sending), for synths that fall behind:
  - **Last-resort skipping while the synth lags** (off by default): once the synth lags over 100 ms behind, quiet note-ons go first, rising to every note-on at 400 ms.
  - **Always skip notes quieter than velocity N** (default 20), with their note-offs.
  - **Collapse stacked notes**: one voice per channel, key and tick, lasting until the last of the stacked notes ends. RFTMIS rev10 sends 42 % fewer note-ons.
  - **Limit sending to N messages/s**: looks 50 ms ahead and skips the quietest note-ons first so the rest fit; within the last velocity it thins evenly, so songs with one velocity still play partly. Note-ons stay within N/2 per second; total messages average N but can briefly reach about 1.5 N as earlier notes end.
- When one thread cannot decode a dense passage in time, decoding spreads over more threads, up to Performance > Playback's "Decoding threads: up to" (0: auto, half the logical processors; a change applies at once), and back to one when the passage ends. The visuals stay exact; audio may differ slightly from one thread's: with collapsing on, a tick split between threads can sound a key once per thread, and the send budget is shared between them. Night of Nights' busiest second (224 M events) decodes in 0.18 s on 16 threads with collapsing, the budget and the velocity floor on, against 0.96 s on one.
- **Start songs paused after loading** (Performance > Playback, off by default).
- Mute, volume, pause, stop and seeking behave as in the original. The metronome and the number-key channel muting and hiding are gone.
- Seeking restores the song's state at the new position, wherever you came from:
  - It resets all controllers, then sends each channel's last value before that point of every controller, program, pitch bend and channel pressure, in the order the song set them, so bank selects still come before their program changes.
  - Volume and pan go back to the GM defaults (100 and 64) on channels with no value yet. So do bank, program, reverb and chorus on channels that only set them later in the song.
  - It looks back only until every value it needs is found, which usually takes microseconds.

**Pre-Render** (Preferences > Audio > Pre-Render, off until its subtab is chosen; each setting's details show when the mouse rests on it)
- The subtab holds what every engine shares (the soundfonts, how far ahead, auto-degrade, the limiter) beside a subtab per engine, BASSMIDI, Kestrel and Merlin, each with its own settings; choosing an engine's subtab switches to that engine at once. Kestrel's and Merlin's advanced settings open from their own tabs: Kestrel's all of them, Merlin's the soundfont and output ones it reads (shared with Kestrel).
- Instead of sending MIDI to a synth, the song is made into audio in the background, ahead of where it plays (5 seconds to 12 hours, default 30 seconds), and played through the sound device, so dense passages play every note.
- Engines:
  - **BASSMIDI** on the processor. As a song plays it renders continuously, as Kiva does: each instance is a BASSMIDI stream kept from one block to the next, given the song's events as its audio is made (at their frames, or at most 16 frames, a third of a millisecond, later where they are denser), and starting again only after a seek or a change. The notes can be split by key over several instances (threads), each with its own voice limit (1 to 100,000), so the voices add up across them; the instances are made on threads of their own and summed, an instance is made only once it has had a note, and it rests again two seconds after its voices have died away (the controllers, programs and bends it gets meanwhile are given to it before its next note). On 9KX2 at 12 key groups split by channel (120 voices each, 10,000 notes a second each; measured with each channel on its own instances, before more of the voices could be used), a second of audio took 45 to 70 ms, and the audio matched the pieces' (0.998 correlation, the same loudness to 0.1 %). On Last Brutal Sister Flandre S (200 million notes), playing from 100 s with the same settings, it stayed 10 to 17 seconds ahead through the densest part without degrading. Offline (the video's audio) and the blocks made again in full are still pieces, several at once.
    - BASSMIDI's pieces are played into a live BASSMIDI instance, their events read back from the piece's MIDI, as the continuous render plays the song. BASSMIDI reading the piece's file itself let notes go short after their note-offs: a chord held by the sustain pedal at a song's end died within half a second instead of ringing on for seconds. Reading the file is kept only for a bassmidi.dll without live instances (tried a few times, a little apart, as BASSMIDI now and then turned a file down with BASS error 0 and no reason, or ran out of memory for a very dense piece's event list); the error then says the piece's size and the rate. A piece BASSMIDI cannot give its soundfonts is made again rather than played silent. The continuous render tries an instance it cannot make three times, then shows the error and starts again, where before that instance's notes went silent while the buffer still filled. A piece cut short for its size (over 192 MB of MIDI) no longer garbles the note-offs written after the cut.
  - A program that none of the soundfonts has (in bank 0) plays their piano (bank 0, program 0; or the last soundfont's first instrument if none has a piano), as Kestrel and Merlin do, instead of nothing: with a piano-only soundfont, a song whose channels choose other instruments was silent. Drums are left as they are.
  - **Split by channel as well as key** (off by default), after OmniMIDI's delegation: 16 x the threads' instances, each with the voice limit. Every channel's keys go round all of them in turn (as the keys do without it), each channel starting one instance further on, so one key's channels go to different instances. Giving each channel its own sixteenth of the instances, as before, left a song's busiest channel on those few: on 9KX2 one channel carries half the voices, so at 48 instances of 1,000 voices only 18,300 sounded on average where the song wanted 57,000; spread over all of them, 43,100 do (as many as split by key alone). Instances that get no notes cost nothing, and the instances are rendered on a few threads (as many as there are cores but two). BASSMIDI renders many small instances much faster than a few large ones: on 9KX2 (14 channels), 60 seconds at 14,000 voices in all took 42.9 s split by key over 14 instances of 1,000, 31.0 s split by channel (14 of 1,000) and 21.9 s split by channel and 4 key groups (56 of 250); 56 instances of 1,000 (56,000 voices) took 37.2 s. Interpolation and how voices over the limit are killed (which OmniMIDI and Maestro set) made no difference.
  - The BASSMIDI tab shows the voice limit in all, worked out as the fields are typed in: "Voice limit: 23,040 (120 x 192 instances)", the voices per instance times the instances (16 to a key group when split by channel, at most 16 groups then). The Merlin tab notes only Merlin's own limits (and the open song's use of them).
  - **Kestrel** and **Merlin** on the graphics card, one piece at a time. Kestrel renders continuously (Maestro's way): one synth is kept from one block to the next and the events are pushed to it as it goes, instead of a synth, a MIDI file and a preroll for each piece; it starts again only after a seek or a change. A second of audio took the graphics card 137 ms instead of 211 in pieces while playing 9KX2 at 160,000 voices; on Last Brutal Sister Flandre S (200 million notes) at 160,000 voices, playing from 100 s, pieces left 16.7 s of silence after the first 5 seconds and the continuous render none. Where it cannot be in time (after a seek into a dense part), it starts a few seconds ahead rather than chasing the playback position. Stacked copies are merged as Kestrel's own coalescing merges them: a note-on's copies that also end on the same frame are one voice at their summed loudness (it reads 4 seconds ahead to find the offs); on a 40-layer test, 76,440 copies were merged and the audio was the same as with every copy to within rounding. What is made again in full, and Merlin's pieces, are still pieces. Merlin has no voice limit and hands what it cannot do to Kestrel (some soundfonts whose envelopes are too long for it, such as GeneralUser GS). It takes one soundfont, so with several it plays the lowest in the list it takes (the one that overrides the rest), alone, and the Pre-Render page says which; only when it takes none of them does Kestrel make every piece. With GeneralUser GS and the Roland Grand Piano in either order, Merlin played the Roland alone, exactly as with the Roland as the only soundfont. Kestrel's advanced settings (interpolation, voice stealing, release floor, filters, LFOs, modulation envelopes, layers, the DC blocker, the block size, and its performance settings: gate resolution, workgroup size, render workgroups, voices per submission, voice building threads, voice sorting, compensated summation, unchecked shaders) are under its tab's Advanced...; Merlin's tab's Advanced... has the soundfont and output ones (filters, LFOs, modulation envelopes, layers, the DC blocker, the block size), which the two share. Kestrel picks a discrete graphics card first, and Vulkan before DirectX 12 on the same card, so it falls back to DirectX 12 where Vulkan is missing.
  - **Warnings:** what a soundfont load reports (an SFZ's unsupported opcodes, samples that would not open) shows on screen once and on the Pre-Render page. Merlin is tried with a one-note piece when chosen; when it does not take the soundfonts, that is said, and Kestrel makes every piece at once. Merlin's tab also says what Merlin does not do properly yet (channel volume and expression, CC7 and CC11; many pitch bends render slowly; one soundfont at a time), and whether the open song uses those.
- **Collapse stacked notes losslessly** (Kestrel's and Merlin's tabs, one setting; on by default): identical notes (same channel, key, velocity and tick) are sent to Merlin once, with Spiderweb's repeat mark carrying their count. On a 40-layer test song, Merlin's pieces were 7 times smaller, and the audio differed from sending every copy no more than two ordinary runs differ. Kestrel merges such copies into one voice at their summed loudness itself, for every piece. BASSMIDI always gets every note.
- **Auto-degrade** (on by default). BASSMIDI's continuous render degrades just in time: before each eighth of a second it makes, its spare time (how long until the block being made plays, less what the rest of it takes at the last eighth's speed) sets how much of the coming note-ons are thinned out: up at once when under half a second is spare, the more the further short it is, and slowly down once 2.5 s are spare or it is fast. Before an eighth's notes go in, what they would cost at that thinning (counted by velocity, at what a note-on has cost so far) is checked too, so a dense burst is thinned as far as it needs before it is made rather than after it has fallen behind. Thinned all it can and still short, each instance's voice limit is cut back as a last resort (to 60 % at a time, at most every half second, down to a sixteenth of the limit), and given back once the thinning has eased: a quarter at a time, or doubled every half second while it makes the audio over twice as fast as it plays (it came back so slowly after a start's burst that the releases at a song's end, seconds later, were still being cut). A start after a seek makes only as much preroll and held-note lead-in as there is time for before its first block plays (the held notes are struck again where it starts either way): a start that took its full lead-in (up to 11 s of audio) was given up again and again as the playback passed it, so an overloaded render could stay silent for 5 to 15 s after a seek, with nothing on the stats box to show it was degrading. Each velocity keeps its own share of its notes, evenly spread, the quiet ones less: at the most, a quarter of the loudest and a sixteenth of the quietest, never none, so a passage never goes silent and a song of one velocity is thinned too; a note left out takes its note-off with it, and a start after a seek is thinned from its preroll on. On 9KX2 at 5 times the speed with one instance of 100,000 voices, seeking 18 s past what was made, the audio was back within 2 to 3 s each time, and 1 block of 62 was late; at the same settings as one's own (12 key groups by channel, 120 voices) at normal speed nothing was thinned. Pieces are checked against when it must play (its first block starting, or the block playing ending), using how long pieces have taken (a part per piece and a part per note, learned as it goes). A piece that would be late is made lighter: BASSMIDI skips its quietest notes until the rest fits, Kestrel lowers its voice cap to a share of the most notes held at once, and Merlin, like BASSMIDI, leaves out its quietest notes (it stays Merlin's). Merlin's pieces are timed by a model of their own: about 0.4 s a piece, then about 0.2 ms per thousand note-ons on a dense song; on Last Brutal Sister Flandre S at 3x speed, its pieces left 28 s of silence over 40 s of playing without auto-degrade and none with it. (How many distinct waves the notes use made little difference there: halving them made a piece about a sixth faster, quartering them no faster still.) Kestrel's continuous render lowers its voice cap as it plays instead (Kestrel steals the quietest voices down to it) when a block takes longer than it plays while less than 3 seconds are ready, by how much too long, and raises it again once 5 seconds are ready. A full piece already being made is given up and made again lighter when it will be late. Lighter pieces are made again in full once 5 seconds are ready, where there is time for it. On 9KX2 with BASSMIDI at 100,000 voices on one thread (it needs over 10 s to make each second), the silences after the first 5 seconds of playing went from 9 s to none.
- The degradation shows on the stats box line "Degraded" (Stats > Statistics): what the audio playing was made with ("thinned 40%", "thinned 100%, voices cut", "voices cut", "vel < 64", "20000 voices" for Kestrel's pieces, or "none"), "thinning 80%, catching up" while nothing is ready to play yet, and how many times it was made lighter so far.
- The stats box line "Synth voices" shows, for the continuous renders (BASSMIDI and Kestrel), the voices sounding in the audio playing, of the voice limit it was made with: "8,412 / 23,040", or "8,412 / 13,824 (cut)" when auto-degrade has cut the limit. It is what the song, the thinning and the NPS limit leave, read every eighth of a second as the audio is made (each BASSMIDI instance's count summed). With KDMAPI it is the synth's own count, as before; for pieces (Merlin's, and audio made again in full) it shows N/A.
- An engine, soundfont or sound change starts the continuous render's degrading afresh: before, the thinning and speeds learned for the old one carried over, so the first seconds after a change could play thinned for no reason. A resting BASSMIDI instance kept a controller changed again in the place of its first value, so it was given back before a 31-EDO tuning SysEx or an RPN that came after it in the song; it now goes after them, in the song's order.
- Soundfonts stay loaded when switching engines: Kestrel and Merlin share one load, and the last load of BASSMIDI and of Kestrel are both kept, so switching back is instant. Each soundfont file stays read while it is in the list, so adding or removing another only sends the set to the graphics card again. Audio made by the previous engine is thrown away at once.
- Every engine gets the same notes: the ones Performance > Sending's velocity floor, stack collapsing and send limit leave (last-resort skipping does not apply: rendering ahead never lags).
- **BASSMIDI's NPS limit per instance** (20,000 by default; 0: none), a field of its own, separate from Performance > Sending's send limit: each instance (each thread, or each channel and key group) limits its own notes a second as OmniMIDI's limiter does. A note of velocity v is played while the instance's rate is under v/127 of the limit, so the quietest go first; the rate is its notes in the last second, or its notes in the current millisecond at a second's rate when that is more, which thins out big chords. A skipped note's off is skipped with it. On 9KX2 (20 seconds, 4 instances), 10,000 a second per instance kept 6,554 note-ons a second of its 143,301 and made the audio 5.6 times faster.
- **31-EDO** (Preferences > Misc): the audio is tuned as the MIDI output is. Scale tuning writes the MIDI Tuning Standard's scale/octave messages into each piece, which all three engines read (Merlin since Kestrel commit `a91cd67`; before, Kestrel made Merlin's tuned pieces). Pitch-bend tuning works on all three too. On 40 steps of 31-EDO, Merlin's pitch matched Kestrel's within 0.06 cents. On a test octave of all 31 steps (a piano soundfont), every note was within 8 cents of true 31-EDO, the same as plain 12-tone notes measure against 12-tone pitch with that piano, against about 50 cents off without the tuning.
- Sharing the graphics card with the visuals: Kestrel works in blocks of 1024 frames by default (Kestrel's own is 4096), so the visuals' frames get in between. Once 5 seconds are made, Kestrel and Merlin also rest between pieces, using the card at most 50 % of the time (adjustable). On a dense black MIDI (9KX2) at an uncapped frame rate, the picture kept about 95 % of its frame rate without rendered audio with Kestrel, against about 61 % with 4096-frame blocks and no rest; Merlin kept about 93 %.
- Soundfonts are SF2 or SFZ, layered in list order. An SFZ's FLAC samples are read by BASS's FLAC plugin (bassflac.dll, built in) for BASSMIDI, and by Kestrel itself; without the plugin BASS falls back on Windows' codecs, which were about 20 times slower to load (0.93 s against 0.05 s for a test SFZ) and are missing on some editions of Windows. The FLAC test rendered exactly as its WAV copy.
  - An SFZ whose `#include` paths are made of `#define` variables (`#include "$DIR/$DYN.txt"`, as in Splendid Grand Piano) is one BASSMIDI turns down ("an SFZ #include it could not find", BASS error 7000): BASSMIDI does not expand variables there. Such an SFZ is read by PFAviz++ instead: its includes put in place, its variables expanded and its sample paths made absolute, into a copy BASSMIDI then opens (written to `%TEMP%\PFAviz++` and deleted once read). Only SFZs BASSMIDI turns down so are read this way: of the 6,009 SFZs tried, Splendid Grand Piano was the only one. Loud and Proud Remastered (includes, sample paths with spaces), read this way to check it, rendered exactly as BASSMIDI's own reading of it.
  - BASSMIDI keeps each soundfont open while PFAviz++ runs, every change of the list using the handles already open (a soundfont changed on disk is opened again). Freeing soundfonts as the list changed could crash inside bassmidi.dll: BASSMIDI shares samples between SFZs that use the same sample files (as Loud and Proud Remastered's presets do), so freeing one while another played could free samples still in use. In a test changing the soundfonts 40 times while six instances played, about half the runs crashed; keeping them open, none did. An SFZ's samples are all loaded when it is opened, on the loading thread: BASSMIDI loading an SFZ's sample files as notes need them, from several instances at once, also crashed (Loud and Proud Remastered with 8 instances striking the same keys crashed in 5 runs of 5, as it did now and then in the app on a 31-EDO black MIDI split by channel; loaded first, 8 runs of 8 went through, its 333 MB loading in 0.3 s from the disk cache). This takes the SFZ's samples' memory at once and some seconds from a cold disk. An SF2's samples did not crash this way and are still loaded as they are needed. Other samples are loaded by the instance that needs them rather than BASSMIDI's background loading threads, which crashed more often in these tests; the audio was the same.
  - An SF2's modulators are read by BASSMIDI only: Kestrel and Merlin read a preset's generators and leave its modulators out. A soundfont that opens its filter by a velocity modulator, as QSP-X Keys does (its cutoff 100 Hz, opened up to 12,400 cents by velocity), sounds muffled there; turning off Filters on the Kestrel or Merlin tab plays it unfiltered.
- The soundfonts start loading in the background when PFAviz++ starts (when rendered audio is on), and again as soon as Preferences change them, so a song plays at once: opening 9KX2 with Kestrel from the command line, the first sound came 1.9 s after playback started instead of 4.0 s. Closing PFAviz++ while they load ends it at once.
- The engines' DLLs (bass.dll, bassmidi.dll and bassflac.dll; kestrel_bridge.dll) are built into PFAviz++.exe, which writes them out to `%LOCALAPPDATA%\PFAviz++\engines` the first time they are needed. A build without them looks for them next to PFAviz++.exe.
- **The song's end**: the audio is made 8 s past the song's last event, and when the song ends (the clock stops half a second past it) what was made plays on to there, fading out over its last second, so releases, the sustain pedal and the reverb ring out as on a MIDI synth (which is let go with all notes off) instead of fading out at once as a pause does. Only when it played up to the end: a seek to the end while paused stays silent. The Voices line follows the audio through it and reads 0 once it has played out.
- Each piece starts early so ringing notes sound right, runs 0.3 s past its end with every note still held let go there, and pieces cross-fade where they join. A seek far away or a speed change makes it again from there; the volume is a gain, so moving it does not. A limiter keeps the audio under full scale. The stats box line "Audio buffer" shows how far ahead it is, as minutes and seconds whatever the buffer's length, as the original shows the song's time (the minutes go on past 59: "0:12/0:30", "12:00/90:00"); once the song's end is made, it shows the whole buffer (nothing more is missing). Only what is made with the soundfonts and sound as they are now counts: after a change, what was made before plays on until it is made again, but the buffer starts again from nothing.
- What has played is thrown away at once (only the block before the one playing is kept, for the join), so going back makes it again.
- A long buffer is kept on disk: past 10 minutes ahead, blocks go to a temporary file, and are read back before they play (about 1.4 GB an hour at 48 kHz on disk; at most about 350 MB in memory however long the buffer). The file is deleted when the song closes.
- Closing a song stops its audio at once: the sound device is let go of first (8.7 ms after File > Close File while playing 9KX2 with Kestrel), and the renderer's threads finish what they were doing in the background.
- Piece sizes: a piece's start costs time (its preroll and the held notes struck again), so BASSMIDI makes 1-block pieces only next to the playback position (in time after a seek), 6-block ones further on and 20-block ones from 20 seconds ahead, and the buffer's far end waits for a full piece's worth instead of being made a block at a time. Merlin's pieces are 12 blocks from 20 seconds ahead (each piece's own part of its time shared by more), and next to the playback position (after a seek) its preroll is not taken back to held notes' starts. On 9KX2 at 1,000 voices, a second of audio took 1,970 ms of processor time in 1-block pieces, 650 in 6-block, 520 in 20-block and 400 in 60-block ones; with a 60-second buffer, playing made 2 one-block pieces instead of 32. Rendering BASSMIDI continuously (one stream from start to end) is the limit of longer pieces: it would save about a third of the processor time against 6-block pieces but make one block at a time, and over 4 minutes of 9KX2 the 8 pieces made at once took the same time either way (23.8 s with 6-block pieces, 23.3 s with 20-block ones), so BASSMIDI stayed in pieces then; it now renders continuously as it plays, its instances on threads of their own (above), and offline (the video renderer) its pieces are 20 blocks. Kestrel's pieces were one at a time anyway, so it renders continuously.

**PFA lag simulation** (Misc tab, its details under Settings...; off by default)
- Plays the song the way the original Piano From Above 1.1.0 would lag on this computer: its frame rate, its freezes and its audio. A model of the original's frame (song/PfaLag.h), counted per millisecond of song from its note statistics and the CPU's caches, with costs measured from stock PFA 1.1.0 itself (its frames logged over seventeen black MIDIs up to 184,000 polyphony, from 2,500 fps down to 216-second frames, with the usual `winmm.dll` patch that sends to KDMAPI, and checked against synthetic slams of 128 keys that grow, hold, renew and release polyphony at set rates):
  - Its Logic goes through every event since the last frame in order, sending each with `midiOutShortMsg` (about 0.04 µs of its own work, plus the send). Every note-off walks the whole list of sounding notes, reading each one's event object. So what costs is note-offs times the polyphony at that moment, not polyphony alone: 10,000 notes held cost nothing more while they are held, but renewed by new notes, every one that ends walks all 10,000. A walk is about 1.2 ns a note while the sounding notes fit in L2, 2.0 ns in L3 and 7.4 ns past it (up to 0.5 ns more in a frame whose events overflow L3, as they push the notes out). Each walk is costed at the polyphony it walks, so a mass release (every note ending at one tick) is priced along the falling polyphony: most of its walking is done while the notes are still many, too many for the caches.
  - Before its sends it reads the events coming into view, and after them the events it played, again (about 0.02 µs an event together), so it sends nothing for a moment after a frame is shown and again before the next.
  - Its Render walks the sounding notes and every event in view twice and draws each note as two rects, with a draw call every 50 notes. Once the two outgrow L2, each sounding note costs about 0.3 µs more (each is drawn over the whole view), so a climb to high polyphony slows the frames even while few notes end.
  - Its clock is the time that passed, so a slow frame leaves more song for the next: once a second of song takes more than a second, each frame is longer than the last, as the original freezes (a frame is capped at ten minutes).
- Frames run back to back, so a light song runs at the original's 2,000–2,500 fps, and the stats box shows that frame rate ("PFA sim"). The picture shows the last finished frame, and so do the statistics and graphs, unless "Statistics follow how far its Logic has played" is on (off by default): then they move on through a freeze as the frame's Logic plays, while the picture holds. The audio plays a frame's events as its Logic gets to them, in the original's order: it crawls through a crash for as long as the crash's walks take, then races through the light song time after it at once, and then waits while the frame draws. That gives the original's pattern in a freeze: a burst of music, a gap, a new frame. Its pace follows the work smoothly (128 points a frame, spaced along both the work and the song time), and the original's frames are worked out a quarter second ahead, so the pace runs on from one to the next by itself instead of waiting on this app's own frames. Inside a millisecond it goes by that millisecond's events rather than their exact times, so a tick that ends a hundred thousand notes goes out note by note over the seconds the original takes to walk them (the sound thins out as it does), with notes starting at that tick before or after them by track, as in the original.
- A MIDI message costs 0.05 µs by default (the field takes decimals): stock PFA with the `winmm.dll` patch that sends to KDMAPI, as measured. Without the patch, on Windows 11 builds whose WinMM goes through the new MIDI service, WinMM itself costs 13–28 µs a message for any synth, even Microsoft's GS Wavetable Synth. That also pushes PFA's own data out of the caches between events, so an unpatched PFA lags far more than its send cost alone (type 30 to come close).
- Mild randomness stands in for the rest of the computer:
  - Other programs take a wandering 0–60 % share of L2 and L3 (typically 15 %).
  - Each frame's own work varies by about 8 %, less for long frames.
  - The send path drifts slowly by about 20 %, each message varies, and an occasional message stalls.
  - Every 0.3 s or so, a stall of about 1.5 ms (another thread, a page fault).
- The L2 and L3 sizes default to this computer's; others can be typed in to see how a different CPU would fare. The costs were measured on a Ryzen 5 5500. On another CPU, a half-second benchmark the first time the simulation starts times the original's note-off walk (indices into pointers to scattered event objects) at half of L1, L2 and L3 and at four times L3, and its per-event work, against the same benchmark on the Ryzen 5 5500. Each cost is scaled by its own result: the walks by the caches' and memory's speed at their size, the rest by the core's (its clock and how much it does per clock). The send cost isn't scaled; it's its own setting. On a Ryzen 5 5500 it is taken as is. "Its CPU speed" scales everything on top (200 % is a CPU twice as fast as this one; decimals allowed, from 0.0001 % to a billion %), to imitate another computer. Changed while it runs (Apply), the simulation's settings take effect at once: a frame under way, even a long freeze, ends where its Logic has got to, and the next ones are worked out with the new settings. Against the measured runs, 5-second stretches come within about ±10 % of the original's frame rate (median 3–9 % per song). Frames longer than a second come within 0.7–1.3× when given the same song time on most songs (0.65–1.7× at worst: songs differ in ways the note statistics don't show). A whole freeze varies a lot from run to run: each frame plays the song time the last one took, so small differences grow. Over several runs, lagging 10-second stretches take a median 0.75–0.9× the original's time. It needs the note statistics, which are counted at load time while it is on (a song opened before it was turned on is opened again).
- **Lag time** (a stats box line, off by default): how long the simulated original has been lagging. A frame of a second or more (under 1 fps) is lag, and a lag spike is a run of such frames with less than a second of quicker ones between them. A spike starts where the slowing down into it does: at the first of the frames of 0.75 s or more (under 1.33 fps) that lead straight into its first slow frame; and earlier still when the song's notes a second or polyphony rise suddenly in the 3 s before that (where the dense part begins, which usually brings the lag on): at the frame that played the rise. A rise is notes a second over the next 0.25 s at least twice, and 20,000 more than, those of the second before, or polyphony at least 1.5 times, and 2,000 more than, the most of the second before; the floors keep the ups and downs of a light or steady passage out. It ends the same way: after the frames of 0.75 s or more straight after its last slow frame (the speeding up out of it). The time counts from the start without pausing through the quicker frames between slow ones (with "(lagging)" meanwhile), and stops at the end until the next spike starts it again from 0. The simulation works its frames out over a second ahead (up to 10 s while it is slowing down), so it knows a frame is slow, or leads into a slow one, as it starts, rather than finding out afterwards. Starting at the lead-in moved the spikes' starts about 0.8 s earlier than starting at the first slow frame; a lower cut-off sat on the 2 fps the frames often hover at before a freeze, and made the start jump by seconds from run to run. On 9KX2, a rise moved 3 of 8 spikes' starts (at this computer's speed and at half of it) a further 0.5 to 0.9 s earlier; before the others the notes a second climb gradually. It follows the CPU and its speed: on 9KX2 at this computer's speed, it found 4 spikes of 5.5 to 14.2 s (the 2 to 3 fps stretches before them are not counted); at half speed, 4 spikes of 7 to 48 s; at twice the speed, one of 1.5 s; at 4 times, none.
- With rendered audio, the audio follows the simulation's clock, stretched at the music's own pitch: short overlapping grains (50 ms) are read from where the clock is, each lined up with the waveform of the one before (WSOLA), so a slowed passage plays its notes at their pitches at the clock's pace, a race through what was held plays the music as the clock passes it, and nothing is played again. While the clock stands still (a freeze), the sound fades out over about 0.1 s, as held notes die away. Through 40 s of 9KX2 at half speed, the slowed audio matched the same passage at normal speed in pitch (1.000) and spectrum (0.98 correlation), and the sound cut in and out once, where before it cut in and out 590 times (a buzz, its spectrum 1.5 times too high).
- **Lag timings** (Misc > PFA lag simulation > Lag timings...): the lag spikes of playing the open song from its start, worked out ahead with the settings on the tab and in its Settings... (applied or not), and the note speed, playback speed, mute and Limit FPS as they are. One row per spike: "From 2:08.5 to 2:14.8 lasting 7.4 s", where it starts and ends in the song and how long it lags (as the Lag time line counts it); Copy puts the rows on the clipboard. It skips the light parts: the song is checked in overlapping 0.3 s windows for whether any frame there could come near the lead-in's 0.75 s (its Logic, and its Render with the most polyphony and notes in view it could have, twice over), and where none could, the song and the wall clock go on together, so its frames are not worked out one by one; they are from 5 s before anything heavier (so a spike's lead-in, its sudden rise and its look back are exact). On 9KX2 that takes 0.2 s where every frame takes 6.3 s (41,000 frames of 454,000; 50–130 times as quick on other lag testers). Its spikes differ from frame by frame about as much as two runs with different noise do (on 9KX2, lengths within about 0.5 s; a short spike near the 1 s line can come or go), since the frames meet a dense part at slightly different places. Where a spike ends in the song is the loosest: the frames out of a freeze each play the long time the last one took. Unlike the simulation as it plays (whose frames stop at 10 minutes, so it never freezes for hours), a frame takes as long as the model says: on Dead Zone Ultimate a freeze of about 139 hours on a Ryzen 5 5500, where the cap gave 20 minutes; set up as a Ryzen 5 5600 at 4.85 GHz (32 MB of L3; everything 1.35 times as fast) 103 hours, or 151 with its memory no faster than this one's, against the 114 the original took there (ELBOW: 10.4 or 12.1 hours against 7.2). How a CPU unlike this one would fare depends on its memory as much as its clock, which the speed setting does not tell apart. It stops after 10 years. It needs the note statistics, as the simulation does.
- **No visual lag** (Misc > PFA lag simulation > Settings..., off by default): every frame is drawn at the playhead, as without the simulation, while the sound, the frame rate shown and the lag time are still the simulated original's.

**Statistics**
- Each line of the stats box is chosen in Stats > **Statistics...** (a window that can be made larger); with every line off the box is not drawn. The lines:
  - Time and frame rate (on by default: time). The original's "Score: N/A" line is gone.
  - Notes passed, NPS (note-ons in the last second) and polyphony (note-ons minus the note-offs that release a held note of the same track, channel and key), from 1 ms tables built at load time (on by default; not counted while they are all off). Max NPS and Max Poly, under them by default, are the most of each in any millisecond of the song up to the point played (a peak inside one millisecond doesn't show, as the polyphony line can't show it either).
  - Synth: the audio buffer (Pre-Render: how much is made ahead, "0:12/0:30"), how it is degraded, the velocity skip threshold, and the synth's voices and CPU (KDMAPI).
  - With Stats > **Advanced lines** on (off by default; a config that showed any of them turns it on), the lines for troubleshooting playback: messages sent (handed to the output), skipped and collapsed per second and the sender's lag; the playback pipeline, to see what holds playback back (messages decoded per second, how busy the decoder, with "×N" while N threads decode, and sender threads are, how full the ring between them is, average and peak, and the output's cost per message: a busy decoder with an empty ring is the decoder's limit; a full ring with a busy sender is the output's, and a rising send cost means the synth is pushing back); and the frame time, split into logic, notes, the rest of the scene and Present. Off, they are neither listed nor drawn. The window's details (which lines need a song reopened, playback or KDMAPI) are in its list's hover tip.
- The box's corner, font, size (width × height in pixels, or as at 1080p growing with the window's height), translucent background and text shadow are set in Stats; the shadow follows the corner (to the left in the left corners, up in the bottom ones). The height's box always shows the height the box is drawn at, also when the font's size or "Size as PFA's" sets it. "Size as PFA's" (on by default) keeps the original's size: 156 pixels wide, unscaled, and 16 pixels a line plus 6, so the box grows and shrinks as lines are shown or hidden, as the original's does. Otherwise its height shared by its lines sets the font size, or the font size can be set instead (the height then follows it, so the text is the same size whatever lines are shown); either way the width is the least it takes, and the box widens itself so the text never overlaps. Settings from before keep the size they had.
- **Graphs** (View > Graphs, Ctrl+G; set in Stats > Graphs...): notes per second, polyphony and tempo over the last moments (a millisecond to ten minutes, the time now at the right edge), each in its own box. Each is filled or a line, in its own colour, with its text at the left or the right, in the order you choose from top to bottom. Each scale follows the highest value shown, easing up and down at speeds you set, and its bottom sits at zero or at the lowest value shown. The size set is each graph's plot; its text goes above and its scale beside it, so the box widens and narrows with the scale's values while the plot keeps its size. A vertical scale can show round values beside the plot with faint lines across, on the side away from the boxes' corner (in a left corner, at the right), so the plot never moves as the values' width changes. A bright line (in a colour you choose) can mark the highest value shown, with that value written by it; it rises at once but comes down only once the highest value has stayed under it for a second of song time, so a song whose bursts come and go as the window slides no longer makes it flick between values.

**Look & effects** (Preferences > Look & effects, off until set; one box turns the effects off)
- The **look** (Normal, Flat, Semi-flat or Kiva) is chosen at the top of the tab, apart from the effects: it is drawn with the effects off too (with its own settings in the box with it, such as the semi-flat outlines and the pressed keys' whitening), and the effect profiles neither save nor change which look is chosen. Those settings are kept in the profiles as before.
- Everything is drawn by its light, from 0 (unlit) to 1 (fully lit): a playing note and the key it presses rise to 1 and can fall along a curve while held; a key let go fades out. Every note can also have a resting light.
- For each part (notes, short notes, keys, red bar, background): how dark it is unlit, how much it is lightened when lit, and how much it glows (bloom). How much of a note's light reaches its outline and its inside, so neither swamps the other.
- Flat look (plain keys, flat notes), semi-flat look (keys without depth, pressed ones told apart by colour only; notes one colour, their outlines darker by an amount you set), hollow notes (outlines only), no red bar (Above the keys: the red bar, nothing, or nothing with the keys cut off flat as in Ultralight MIDI Player: the white and black keys start on one line, without the dark line over them or the black keys rising above the white ones; pressed black keys keep their tops on it), and a vignette: darkest in the corners, or from one side only (top, bottom, left or right), over the whole picture or the notes area only (not the keyboard), fading out over as much of the way in as you set.
- **Kiva look**: the keyboard, bar and notes of the [Kiva](https://github.com/arduano/Kiva) MIDI player's Big Keyboard; everything else (background, lines, stats, graphs, the other effects) stays as set. Only the look is Kiva's, not its code.
  - Keyboard 5.98 white keys tall (4% of Kiva's 256-key layout's width), whatever the range shown, up to a quarter of the window as with the other looks. The black keys sit where Kiva puts them (C# and D# 0.15 of a black key off centre, F# and A# 0.25, G# centred), and the notes follow them.
  - The bar is the keyboard's top 6%, in the bar colour (Preferences; Kiva's own default is blue, #0068C9), shading to 80% of it at its bottom, with a soft shadow above it over the notes and one below it over the white keys.
  - White keys meet with a 1-pixel dark line between them, shade from 80% of their colour at the top to all of it, and have a darker front, shorter when pressed. Black keys are black with lighter bevelled sides and front, their faces raised into the bar; pressed, they are the note's colour, the face darker than the bevels, and drop flush.
  - Notes are outlined in a fifth of their colour, the inside going from their colour lightened at the left to darkened at the right; the outline is 0.091% of the window's width (2 pixels at 1920).

- **Profiles**: every effect setting (but the on/off switch and the look chosen) can be saved under a name and picked again, as track palettes are. Save puts the page's settings into the chosen profile (greyed out while they match it), Save as... under a new or another name; saving or deleting a profile is kept at once, without Apply. Defaults shows the default settings (the look chosen kept). A config from before profiles keeps its effects as a profile named "My previous effects". Profiles saved with a look chosen keep it no more: choosing one leaves the look as it is.
- **Merged outlines**: touching notes of one track and channel share one outline, around them all; short notes can stay apart, merge among themselves, or merge with the long notes too.
- **Auto exposure** follows how much of the keyboard is lit, turning the light up in sparse parts and down in dense ones, and a **soft limit** caps the glow added to a pixel so dense parts do not wash out to white.
- **Hit line**: a line in the red bar's place, right on the keys, drawn over everything. It can run across the keyboard or show only over the keys being played, take the notes' colours, fade with its own time and curve after a key is let go, spread sideways, and glow.

**Interface**
- **Dark mode** (Preferences, Interface tab: Light, Dark or Follow Windows), dialogs, menus and list headers included.
- No window opens where its title bar can't be grabbed: the main window, Preferences and every dialog are checked as they open. A title bar not wholly on a screen from top to bottom, or with less than half its width (or 200 pixels) on screen, is moved onto the nearest screen, the whole window with it where it fits. Once open, a window can be dragged anywhere, off the screens too; the main window's position is saved as it would open, so it always comes back on screen. Preferences and the other dialogs (loading, Statistics, Graphs and the rest) open where they were last, and the resizable ones (the loading window and its statistics, Statistics, the video progress window, choosing a MIDI in an archive) at the size they were last given by hand. Places are kept by title as the windows are moved, resized, hidden or closed, saved to the settings within two seconds (so they hold across a crash too), and put back on screen if that is no longer on one; the loading window and the statistics it turns into keep a place each. A main window saved at (100, -300) opens at (100, 0); one dragged to (-900, -450) stays there and is saved at (0, 0).
- Preferences fit the screen: a tab that does not fit (a large display scaling, a small screen) scrolls, so OK and Apply stay in reach. The main window stays usable while Preferences is open.
- **Preferences** are in eight tabs by task: Notes & keys (the keys shown, track colours, note drawing), Stats, Effects, Audio (subtabs KDMAPI, WinMM and Pre-Render, which switch the output as they are chosen), Interface, Performance (loading, drawing, memory, sending and decoding), Video rendering and Misc (the background and bar colours, the background image, 31-EDO, the loading statistics, timing, pairing and the PFA lag simulation). The layouts run down the page, at most two settings to a line, and choices between options are listed one under another. Each tab sets only what it shows, Apply lights only while a tab differs from what it last applied (a change undone, or a dialog closed with nothing changed, leaves it off; a colour picked lights it as any other change does), and each tab has Restore Defaults in the same corner (Audio's keeps the soundfonts, the output and the engine). Rarer settings open in their own dialogs (Clean drawing's Advanced..., the lag simulation's Settings..., the effects' Each part..., Bloom, exposure, vignette... and Hit line...), short labels carry the details in hover tips, and a value a tab cannot take brings that tab to the front.
- Colours of the background, the bar above the keys and the tracks are set in Preferences > Misc (the background and the bar) and Notes & keys (the tracks); the colour dialog takes hex codes (`#RRGGBB` or `#RGB`), as do colour settings in the config file. A changed track palette recolours the open song at once. Track palettes can be saved under a name and picked again. After the palette's colours, channels take them again (*then loop*), random ones as the original does (*then random*), or random ones that never repeat (*then unique*): each differs from every other and from the palette's, drawn from the original's range of bright colours while it has some left, then from all 16.7 million.
- The loading window shows each stage's progress and time (its bars glide to each new value instead of jumping a few times a second), and, when the load is done (Misc > "Show loading statistics after a file is loaded", off by default), a report: the load time, notes, speed and peak memory up top, then each stage's time and the song's tracks, events and notes. Misc > "Show the full loading statistics" (off by default) adds the counting's details while it loads, and to the report each stage's parts, the song's contents, its memory by part, the process's memory and how the file was read, in two columns (one when the window is narrower; narrower than that, it keeps its width and scrolls sideways). Copy stats copies it as text. The last load's report opens again from File > Loading Statistics, whether or not it showed by itself.
- Cancel closes the loading window at once, at any stage. The load stops in the background and frees its memory; a song opened meanwhile waits for that, then loads.

**Video rendering** (File > Render Song..., or Preferences > Video rendering > Start rendering for the open song: the same settings, kept in both)
- Renders the open song from start to end to an MP4, off-screen at any resolution up to 8192 × 8192 and 1–240 fps, with x264 (CPU) or a hardware encoder (NVIDIA NVENC, AMD AMF or Intel QSV; H.264 or HEVC), a quality setting (CRF, CQ or QP; default 20) and a speed preset.
- Frames are converted to YUV 4:2:0 (BT.709) on the GPU and read back a few frames behind the GPU, so ffmpeg only encodes. At 1080p60 on an RTX 3060: about 290 fps with x264 veryfast, 540 fps with NVENC.
- A frame is what the window shows (keyboard and notes with the current view, note speed, colours and hidden channels) plus, optionally, the stats box (its chosen lines, less the synth and frame-time ones).
- Audio, optionally: an audio file, starting at the song's 0 s mark (the MIDI's start, where a render of it starts, so notes that make no sound at the start cannot throw it off), then moved by an offset from -60 to +60 seconds, moved by "Audio offset" (its range in its hover tip), or the audio renderer's own audio ("From Pre-Render (uses its settings)"), made with the Pre-Render page's engine, soundfonts and settings and the playback sound settings (velocity floor, volume), from the video's first frame on, so it is in time with the video by construction. It is always made from scratch: what was rendered ahead for playback is thrown away and made again after the video. Kestrel and Merlin make it as their own whole renders, the song (up to 10 minutes at a time) in one piece each, so it has no joins: on 2 minutes of 9KX2, Kestrel's was the same as its continuous render, sample for sample, and as fast (23.6 s), and Merlin's took 6.1 s instead of 7.4 s in 16-second pieces (its own time 2.8 s instead of 4.2 s) and differed from them only for a few seconds after each of their joins. BASSMIDI makes it in 20-second pieces, several at once. It is made while the frames are rendered, with its own progress bar (as the engine reports it, for a whole render), then put into the video (AAC in MP4 and MOV, FLAC in MKV, PCM in AVI and ProRes MOV); cancelling makes it up to where the video stopped. This works with rendered audio turned off for playback too.
- **End**: the video ends a set time after the song's last event (0 to 60 s, 0.5 s by default as before), its audio fading out over the last of it (a second at most) instead of stopping dead; or when its audio ends, but never before the last event: an audio file's own length (with its offset), or the Pre-Render audio, made up to 8 s past the last event and cut where its sound has died away (below -80 dB). Without audio it ends as the first choice says. On a test MIDI (a pedalled chord ending at 1 s, with 3 s of lead-in) the videos came out 6.0 s with a 1 s fade (2 s after), 12.0 s (until the Pre-Render audio ends, the pedal never lifted) and 9.0 s (a 6 s audio file).
- Needs `ffmpeg.exe` in the current folder, next to `PFAviz++.exe`, or on the PATH. The progress window looks like the loading window: the output's name and folder, what it is doing, a preview of the latest frame (taking the room the window is given), the time elapsed, the video's frames a second and speed against real time, the audio's speed and the time left (each part's figures stop where they ended once it is done), smooth bars for the video, the audio, putting the audio into the video (ffmpeg's progress) and all of it together, and the song time, frame size, encoder and the audio engine's speed. Cancel keeps a playable video of the part done so far.

**31-EDO** (Preferences > Misc > 31-EDO; the layout: Notes & keys)
- **31-EDO keyboard layout (256 key mode only)**: the 256-key view laid out as a 31-EDO keyboard, 31 keys an octave, with the half sharps and flats as their own tiers of keys.
- 31-EDO tuning retunes the synth (by MIDI Tuning Standard messages) so keys past 127 play, each at its 31-EDO pitch; it applies at once. Off, keys past 127 are not played. A pitch-bend tuning, for synths without MTS, is there too but does not work yet with MIDI synths; with Pre-Render both work (see Pre-Render).

**Settings and the window**
- The notes and keyboard area's exact size can be typed in under Interface > View size, up to its monitor's size less the visible title, frame, menu and controls bars (which are added around it; Windows' invisible resize borders don't count). The window then moves, if it has to, so its visible frame lies wholly on that monitor. The size the page shows is always accepted as it is, even maximized or in full screen.
- Settings are kept in `%APPDATA%\PFAviz++\config.ini`. Several versions can share it, even running at once: each saves only the settings it changed and keeps the ones it does not know, and every setting is also kept in `config.all.ini` beside it, so one an older version dropped comes back with the next newer one.
- Environment variables for timed test runs: `PFAVIZ_MUTE=1`, `PFAVIZ_UNCAPPED=1`, `PFAVIZ_NOTESPEED=x`, `PFAVIZ_MAXIMIZE=1`, `PFAVIZ_FRAMELOG=file` (frame times every half second), `PFAVIZ_GDITEXT=1` (the GDI text fallback even with D3DX installed), `PFAVIZ_START=seconds` (starts that far into the song), `PFAVIZ_SPEED=x` (plays at x times), `PFAVIZ_EXIT_AFTER=seconds` (closes the window without saving the configuration), `PFAVIZ_AUDIOCAP=file` (the rendered audio as played, before volume and limiter, raw 32-bit float stereo, with a timing line per stretch in file.txt), `PFAVIZ_RAMSECS=n` (rendered audio: only n seconds ahead kept in memory, the rest on disk), `PFAVIZ_KSTREAM=0` (Kestrel in pieces instead of its continuous render), `PFAVIZ_BSTREAM=0` (BASSMIDI in pieces as it plays, as before its continuous render), `PFAVIZ_RUNBLOCKS=n` (offline pieces of n blocks), `PFAVIZ_RENDERLOG=file` (a line per rendered piece: when, which blocks, how long it took, its notes, whether it was made lighter, the time predicted and left; with `PFAVIZ_PIECEDUMP=folder`, each piece's MIDI too) and `PFAVIZ_RENDER=out.mp4` (renders the song with the saved video settings, then closes; with `PFAVIZ_ENCODER=n`, the n-th encoder of the list instead, `PFAVIZ_CAPTUREDEPTH=n` frames in flight, `PFAVIZ_BGRXPIPE=1` BGRX frames converted by ffmpeg).
